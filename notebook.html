How the study was actually built

The decisions that shaped the analysis, the reasoning behind each, and what it cost. Where a choice weakened the study, it is marked as such.

9.2Decisions & rationale

Dichotomise burnout at the median

Why: to enable binary classifiers and stay comparable with prior burnout-classification work. Median split gives roughly balanced classes (181 / 140).
Cost: throws away information in the continuous score and manufactures well-separated classes that make the task easier — a direct contributor to the very high scores. Flagged on Limitations #5.

Restrict features to four continuous composites

Why: a small, theoretically grounded feature set keeps the model interpretable and the SHAP story legible; all four map onto established constructs.
Cost: excludes potentially useful predictors; narrow feature space can also make separation look cleaner than it would with noisier real-world inputs.

Exclude sleep and screen time

Why: both were collected only in categorical bands; mixing them into a continuous, z-scored feature set without a principled encoding was judged worse than leaving them out.
Cost: loses theoretically relevant signal. Re-including them with ordinal encoding is an explicit next step.

Reverse-score the efficacy items into the burnout composite

Why: in the burnout framework, reduced academic efficacy is a burnout dimension, so low efficacy should raise the composite. Items were recoded (6 − raw) before averaging.
Cost: self-efficacy also appears as a separate predictor, so the two share item-level content by design — a point to keep in mind when reading its coefficient.

Complete-case analysis

Why: item-level missingness was under 1%; imputation would add complexity for negligible gain.
Cost: minimal here; would not scale to a dataset with real missingness.

Fit standardisation inside each CV fold

Why: fitting the scaler on the whole dataset before splitting leaks test-fold statistics into training — one of the most common causes of silently inflated cross-validation scores.
Cost: none; this is the correct default.

Keep the two-item protective scales despite low alpha

Why: Cronbach’s alpha is mathematically suppressed for two-item scales regardless of item quality; the constructs are theoretically essential and their effects held up in both analyses.
Cost: genuine measurement imprecision remains and probably understates the protective effects. Validated multi-item scales are the fix.

Compare four algorithms across the complexity spectrum

Why: running Logistic Regression alongside SVM, Random Forest and XGBoost answers whether nonlinear flexibility actually buys predictive gain here, and provides a cross-model consistency check on the feature ranking.
Cost: none; strengthens the conclusion.

Compute SHAP on the Random Forest with TreeExplainer

Why: Random Forest was the best model; TreeExplainer gives exact SHAP values efficiently for tree ensembles, over all 321 observations.
Cost: the explanation is specific to that model; a different top model could shift local attributions.

Report mean and SD across the five folds

Why: a single split is noisy; fold-averaged metrics with dispersion are the minimum honest summary.
Cost: still one sample and one CV seed — repeated CV across seeds is not yet done.

9.3Considered, not pursued
  • Continuous-outcome modelling (regression / ordinal ML on the raw burnout score) instead of a median split.
  • Repeated cross-validation across random seeds to quantify run-to-run variance, plus hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.
  • VIF / ridge / SEM to handle the stress–distress collinearity explicitly rather than noting it.
  • Validated multi-item instruments (MSPSS, full academic self-efficacy subscale, complete MBI-SS or SBI).
  • External validation on an independent cohort — the single most important missing piece.

These are recorded here rather than buried so a reader knows they were consciously deferred, not overlooked.
