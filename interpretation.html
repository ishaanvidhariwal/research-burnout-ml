07  —  Interpretation

What the findings mean — and what they don’t

Two analyses, run on different principles, tell one story. That convergence is the finding. The size of the numbers is not.

7.1The primacy of psychological distress

Psychological distress was the strongest predictor of burnout in both the regression (B = 0.356, p < .001) and the SHAP analysis (mean |SHAP| = 0.231 — over four times the contribution of academic stress). This fits the classic account of burnout as progressive emotional resource depletion: students who struggle to regulate emotion and cope under sustained pressure exhaust the reserves that academic engagement draws on faster than those facing domain-specific academic stress alone.

It also aligns with the moderated-mediation model in which academic anxiety carries much of stress’s effect on burnout. Distress — which spans anxiety plus broader emotional strain — looks like the more proximal, more complete predictor. Practically: monitoring only academic workload will miss students whose burnout is driven by distress originating outside the classroom.

7.2Support and efficacy as protective factors

Social support had the second-largest SHAP contribution (0.163) and an independent regression effect (B = −0.314, p < .001). For students in a high-pressure examination culture, believing that family support is available and knowing where to seek help appears to buffer the depletion process.

Self-efficacy was likewise independently protective (B = −0.308, p < .001; mean |SHAP| = 0.110), replicating prior work in which stronger academic self-efficacy attenuates burnout under stress. Higher efficacy likely reframes demands as manageable challenges rather than threats, lowering the emotional strain that, sustained, becomes exhaustion and cynicism.

Together this supports a risk-and-resilience picture: burnout risk depends not just on stressors present but on protective resources available.

7.3Why academic stress attenuates

Academic stress correlates with burnout on its own (r = .441, p < .001) but is not a unique predictor once distress is in the model (B = −0.096, p = .107), and it has the smallest SHAP value (0.056). The most parsimonious reading is the r = .683 overlap between stress and distress: entered together, distress absorbs the burnout-relevant variance that stress carried alone. Consistent with a mediation account, stress may act on burnout largely through distress rather than beside it. This is a statement about these four variables in one model — not evidence that academic stress is unimportant.

7.4Reading the SHAP results

The SHAP summary plot adds direction to the importance ranking:

  • higher psychological distress → pushes the prediction toward high burnout;
  • higher academic stress → small push toward high burnout;
  • higher social support → pushes toward low burnout;
  • higher self-efficacy → pushes toward low burnout.

Every direction matches the regression sign. That the interpretable linear model and the game-theoretic explanation of a nonlinear ensemble agree is the strongest internal consistency check available here.

The boundary of the claim SHAP explains the model. "Distress has mean |SHAP| 0.231" means the Random Forest relied heavily on distress when sorting these 321 students. It is not a dose–response curve and not a causal effect estimate. No intervention claim follows directly from a SHAP value.
7.5Why is the performance so high?

A ROC-AUC of .998 warrants scrutiny rather than celebration. Several things are going on, and it is worth separating the safeguards that are in place from the reasons the number is nonetheless inflated.

Safeguards in place

  • Standardisation (StandardScaler) was fit on training folds only, so no test-fold statistics leaked into preprocessing.
  • Folds were stratified; every observation was scored exactly once as held-out.
  • The target is a function of the burnout items only; the four predictors are separate scales, so the outcome was not fed back in as a feature.
  • Duplicate submissions were removed before analysis.
  • Four independent algorithms — and the separate regression — agree, which a single-model leak would not typically produce.

Why the number is still inflated

  • Median-split target. Splitting at the median manufactures two balanced, well-separated groups. Cases near the cut are rare by construction, so the boundary is unusually clean.
  • Modest N. With 321 rows, cross-validated estimates can track sample-specific structure that would not recur in new data.
  • Single sample, no external validation. Nothing here tests the model on data it has never seen from a different cohort.
  • Self-report, same instrument. Predictors and outcome come from one questionnaire completed in one sitting, sharing method variance.

The honest summary: the pipeline avoids the obvious leakage traps, but the headline metrics should be read as an optimistic upper bound under favourable conditions. The claim this study can stand behind is the ranking and direction of predictors, which is stable across methods — not that a deployable 97%-accurate burnout detector exists.

7.6Practical implications
  • Screen for wellbeing, not just workload. Distress — broader than academic stress — is the strongest signal. Wellbeing screening that captures general emotional burden will catch students that a workload-only lens misses.
  • Build protective resources, don’t only cut stressors. Social support and self-efficacy are independently protective; mentoring, peer networks, visible counselling pathways, family engagement, and efficacy/coping skill-building are plausible levers alongside load management.
  • Short instruments can carry real signal. A brief, interpretable model is feasible — but only after longitudinal, externally validated work replaces this proof-of-concept.
Not a deployment recommendation Nothing here should be used to screen, label, or triage real students. The design is cross-sectional, the sample is small and non-representative, and the model is unvalidated outside this dataset.
