02  —  The Study

How the question was investigated

A single anonymous survey, six psychosocial measures, and a design that trades depth for reach — a cross-sectional convenience sample of 321 students.

2.1Participants

N = 321. Students currently enrolled in Grades 9–12 (secondary school) or a first-year undergraduate programme, recruited through school networks, social-media platforms, and peer referral in India. The only inclusion criterion was current enrolment at one of these levels.

This is a convenience sample. It was not randomly drawn from any defined population, so it cannot be assumed to represent Indian students as a whole, and findings should not be generalised to other education systems without replication. The Limitations page treats sampling in detail.

2.2Measures

All items used a five-point Likert-type scale. Each construct score is the unweighted mean of its items; higher scores mean more of the named construct. Full item wording is in Appendix A of the manuscript.

Measurement caveat The burnout composite is only of questionable internal consistency (α = .655), and the two protective scales have low alpha because two-item scales suppress the statistic regardless of item quality. Limited measurement precision most likely understates the protective factors’ contributions. This is discussed on Limitations.

Why psychological distress is separate from academic stress

Academic stress is domain-specific: it is about schoolwork, grades, and exams. Psychological distress is broader — general trouble regulating emotion, persistent worry, and coping under pressure from any source. Measuring them separately is what makes it possible to ask whether burnout is driven by the academic load specifically, or by a wider emotional burden. The results point to the latter.

2.3Procedure
  • The survey was administered electronically via Google Forms and distributed through school and undergraduate contacts, social media, and peer referral.
  • Collection continued until 321 usable responses were obtained after removing duplicate submissions identified through response-pattern analysis.
  • No monetary or academic compensation was offered.
  • Composite scores were computed as item means. For the burnout composite, the two academic-efficacy items were reverse-scored (6 − raw) before averaging, so that higher values indicate lower efficacy and therefore greater burnout.
  • Complete-case analysis was used: a participant missing any item on a given scale was dropped from analyses involving that scale. With missingness under 1%, the effect on statistical power was negligible.
2.4Ethics & privacy

The study involves minors and asks about emotional wellbeing, so the safeguards matter. What the consent statement told every participant before they began:

  • the study’s purpose is academic research only;
  • participation is entirely voluntary, with an unrestricted right to withdraw at any point without consequence;
  • all responses are anonymous and confidential;
  • no personally identifiable information is collected at any stage;
  • responses will not be shared with teachers, parents, or any institutional body;
  • general mental-health resources are available should any item cause distress.
🔒
The raw participant data is not published on this site. Because responses come from students and include wellbeing items, the individual-level dataset is kept private. What is shared instead: the full data dictionary, aggregate statistics and distributions, a small synthetic sample with the same structure, and the analysis code. Aggregate results are reported throughout with no cell small enough to risk re-identification.

This was independent student research. It was conducted following standard ethical practice for anonymous, minimal-risk survey research — informed consent, no identifying data, voluntary participation, withdrawal rights — but did not undergo review by an institutional ethics board.
