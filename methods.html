04  —  The Method

How the question was tested

A deliberately simple pipeline: four features, four classifiers of increasing complexity, honest cross-validation, and SHAP to explain the winner.

4.1Target variable

The continuous burnout composite was dichotomised at the sample median (3.29). Students at or above the median form the High Burnout class (n = 181, 56.4%); those below form the Low Burnout class (n = 140, 43.6%).

A median split makes binary classification possible and follows prior burnout-classification work, but it discards information in the continuous score and produces two well-separated classes. Both consequences are treated as limitations on the Limitations page and revisited in Interpretation.

4.2Feature set & standardisation

Four continuous composite scores were used as features: academic stress, psychological distress, self-efficacy, social support. No demographic, sleep, or screen-time variables were included in the primary models.

All features were standardised to zero mean and unit variance (StandardScaler), fit inside each training fold only and then applied to the held-out fold. This prevents information from the test fold leaking into preprocessing — the most common way cross-validated scores get quietly inflated.

Spanning linear to ensemble models answers a specific question: does the extra flexibility of tree ensembles buy meaningful predictive gain over a linear model here? (Experiments — the answer is "a little".)

4.4Validation & metrics

Every model was evaluated with five-fold stratified cross-validation: the data is split into five folds that each preserve the 56/44 class ratio, each fold serves as the test set exactly once, and results are averaged. Mean and standard deviation across folds are reported.

  • Accuracy — overall proportion correct.
  • Precision — of students flagged high-burnout, the share who truly are.
  • Recall — of truly high-burnout students, the share flagged.
  • F1 — harmonic mean of precision and recall.
  • ROC-AUC — ranking quality across all thresholds.
4.5SHAP explainability

For the best model (Random Forest), SHAP values were computed with TreeExplainer for all 321 observations. SHAP, grounded in cooperative game theory, attributes each prediction to its features additively — giving both a global importance ranking (mean absolute SHAP per feature) and local explanations for individual students.

What SHAP is and isn’t SHAP describes how features moved the model’s output. It is a statement about the model, not about causation in the world. A large SHAP value for distress means the model leaned heavily on distress — not that reducing distress would reduce burnout by a corresponding amount.
4.6Software

Analyses were run in Python 3.11 using scikit-learn (models, CV, scaling), XGBoost, and the shap library; the statistical analyses used statsmodels and pingouin. Versions and a one-command run are on Reproduce the Work.
