08  —  Limitations & Next Steps

Where the evidence stops

Eight constraints bound what can be claimed. Naming them plainly is part of the result — and each one points to a concrete next study.

1. Cross-sectional design — no causal inference

Every variable was measured once. It is impossible to tell whether distress, low support, or low efficacy precede burnout or whether burnout produces them through feedback. Next: longitudinal measurement across an academic year to establish temporal order.

2. Self-report only

All measures are self-reported and subject to social desirability, recall distortion, and mood-congruent responding. Next: triangulate with academic records, teacher ratings, attendance, or physiological stress markers.

3. Non-representative sample

A convenience sample of Indian secondary and first-year undergraduate students. Academic pressure, support infrastructure, and disclosure norms vary widely across systems, so the pattern may not transfer. Next: replication across multiple national contexts.

4. Low reliability on three scales

Self-efficacy (α = .265) and social support (α = .572) are two-item scales; the burnout composite is questionable (α = .655). Limited precision most likely understates the protective factors. Next: validated multi-item instruments (e.g. MSPSS for support; a full academic self-efficacy subscale; the complete MBI-SS or SBI for burnout).

5. Performance-inflating design choices

Binarising burnout at the median creates two balanced, separable classes, and N = 321 is modest for machine learning, so cross-validated scores may not generalise. Next: model burnout continuously, use a population-referenced threshold, and validate externally on an independent sample.

6. Stress–distress collinearity

The r = .683 correlation between academic stress and distress can inflate standard errors and suppress the apparent unique contribution of stress. Next: VIF diagnostics, ridge regression, or SEM that models the shared variance explicitly.

7. Burnout treated as one dimension

Exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy were collapsed into a single composite, obscuring dimension-specific effects. Next: full instruments scored as separate subscales, analysed individually.

8. Excluded variables

Sleep duration and screen time were collected but left out of the models because of their categorical encoding, despite being theoretically relevant. Next: re-include them with ordinal encoding; add sleep quality, academic performance, and peer relationships to the feature set.

Bottom line This is preliminary, proof-of-concept work. It shows that a small set of psychometric items carries a strong, consistent burnout signal and that the signal can be explained. It does not establish causation, generalisability, or a deployable screening tool.
