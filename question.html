01  —  The Question

Why I investigated this

Most burnout research explains relationships at the group level. Far less of it tries to identify which individual students are heading toward burnout — early enough for support to matter.

The research question

Can academic burnout among secondary school and first-year undergraduate students be predicted from psychological and academic factors — and can the resulting model be made interpretable enough to act on?

The study pairs two goals that are often treated separately. The first is prediction: training machine-learning classifiers to sort students into higher- and lower-burnout groups from a short set of survey measures. The second is explanation: using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) so that a prediction comes with a reason — which factors pushed a given student toward the high-burnout classification, and by how much.

Why this question matters

Academic burnout — chronic emotional exhaustion, cynicism toward studies, and a reduced sense of academic competence — is associated with lower performance, weaker motivation, higher dropout intention, and elevated risk of depression and anxiety. It tends to build cumulatively rather than appear suddenly, and it peaks around the transitions between school and university, when demands and disruptions converge.

Interventions work best when they are targeted and early. Without a way to flag risk in advance, schools are left responding to burnout after it is already visible in grades and attendance. A short, interpretable screening model is a modest but practical step toward getting ahead of that.

The sample is also deliberately chosen. Secondary and early-undergraduate students in India face intense examination competition and high achievement expectations, yet they are under-represented in the machine-learning-in-education literature, which skews toward Western university populations.

Background in brief

The study is framed by four bodies of theory, covered in full on the literature review:

  • Burnout as resource depletion (Maslach & Jackson, 1981; Maslach et al., 2001) — burnout develops from a sustained mismatch between demands and coping resources.
  • Self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1977, 1997) — belief in one's academic capability shapes whether demands are appraised as challenges or threats.
  • Control-Value Theory (Pekrun, 2006) — achievement anxiety emerges when stakes are high but perceived control is low, and it drains self-regulatory resources over time.
  • A moderated-mediation model of stress and burnout (Gao et al., 2023) — academic stress feeds burnout partly through anxiety, and self-efficacy buffers that path.

Directional predictions

The study was not pre-registered and did not state formal numbered hypotheses. The four directional predictions below follow from the theory above; the analyses were designed to evaluate them. Verdicts summarise what the results showed.

H1
Higher psychological distress is associated with a greater likelihood of burnout.
Verdict — supported: strongest unique predictor (B = 0.356, p < .001; mean |SHAP| = 0.231).
H2
Higher academic stress is associated with a greater likelihood of burnout.
Verdict — partially supported: strong on its own (r = .441, p < .001) but not a unique predictor once distress is included (B = −0.096, p = .107).
H3
Greater self-efficacy is associated with a lower likelihood of burnout.
Verdict — supported: B = −0.308, p < .001; mean |SHAP| = 0.110.
H4
Greater social support is associated with a lower likelihood of burnout.
Verdict — supported: B = −0.314, p < .001; mean |SHAP| = 0.163.
Scope note Every relationship in this study is observational and cross-sectional. "Predict" here means statistical prediction within a dataset, not a claim that any factor causes burnout. See Interpretation.
